Unit 1 Introduction to Forensic Medicine +
1.1 Core Definitions
TermDefinition
Forensic MedicineApplication of medical sciences to legal purposes to aid administration of justice. Also called Legal Medicine or State Medicine.
Medical JurisprudenceFrom Latin juris (law) + prudentia (knowledge). Application of legal knowledge to medical practice. Covers doctor–patient, doctor–doctor, and doctor–State relationships.
Clinical Forensic MedicineBranch dealing with living persons — clinical and therapeutic info alongside forensic principles.
Forensic PathologyInvestigation of sudden, unexpected, and/or violent deaths; determines cause of death.
ThanatologyScientific study of death and its related phenomena.
ToxicologyScience dealing with poisons — their detection, actions, and antidotes.
1.2 Branches of Forensic Medicine
  • Forensic Pathology: Investigation of unnatural/sudden deaths, autopsies
  • Clinical Forensic Medicine: Examination of living persons (assault victims, sexual offences)
  • Forensic Toxicology: Chemical analysis, poison identification, drug testing
  • Forensic Odontology: Dental identification, bite-mark analysis
  • Forensic Psychiatry: Mental state examination, fitness to plead
  • Forensic Anthropology: Skeletal remains, age/sex/race determination
  • Forensic Serology/DNA: Blood grouping, DNA fingerprinting, paternity disputes
1.3 Doctor's Legal Roles
RoleImplication
As a WitnessCalled to testify as an expert in court; opinion evidence permissible
As a ProfessionalBound by professional duties — confidentiality, duty of care, informed consent
As a State AgentObligated to report certain conditions (notifiable diseases, medico-legal cases) to authorities
1.4 Medico-Legal Case (MLC)

Definition: Any case in which the attending doctor, after examining a patient, considers that police/court involvement is necessary.

  • Road traffic accidents, assault, poisoning, burns, sexual offences, unnatural deaths
  • A doctor who fails to report an MLC can be charged under BNS (formerly IPC)
  • First-aid cannot be refused — treat patient first, then inform police
  • All findings must be documented contemporaneously in the MLC register

Refusing to treat an MLC patient is a criminal offence in India. Treatment must be provided even without police intimation. Always obtain an MLC number.

Unit 3 Thanatology — Death & Its Types +
3.1 Types of Death
TypeDefinitionKey Points
Somatic (Clinical) DeathCessation of vital functions: heartbeat, respiration, consciousnessReversible early on (CPR, defibrillation); brain survives 4–6 min after cardiac arrest
Cellular (Molecular) DeathIrreversible death of individual cells/tissuesOccurs hours after somatic death; different cells survive different durations
Brain DeathComplete irreversible cessation of all brain functions including brainstemLegally recognised under THOTA 1994; enables organ donation; 2 confirmatory tests 6h apart
Apparent DeathVital functions depressed but not absent — mimics deathCauses: profound hypothermia, drug overdose, syncope, catalepsy; danger of premature burial
3.2 Brain Death — Criteria (India, THOTA 1994)
CriterionAssessment
Deep unreactive comaGlasgow Coma Scale = 3; no response to any stimulus
Absent brainstem reflexesNo pupillary, corneal, gag, cough, oculocephalic, oculovestibular reflexes
Absent spontaneous breathingApnoea test: no respiratory effort at PaCO₂ ≥60 mmHg
Known irreversible causeDrug effects, metabolic causes, hypothermia must be excluded
Confirmation intervalTwo separate assessments ≥6 hours apart by two senior doctors (one a neurologist/neurosurgeon)

Brain death enables organ donation under THOTA 1994. It is the legal equivalent of death in India. Time of death = time of second assessment confirming brain death.

3.5 Sudden Natural Death
  • Definition: Unexpected death from natural disease in a person who appeared healthy within 24 hours
  • Most common cause: Ischaemic heart disease (coronary artery disease) — responsible for >50% of SDN
  • Others: Pulmonary embolism, aortic rupture, subarachnoid haemorrhage, epilepsy, bronchial asthma
  • Medico-legal: autopsy mandatory if circumstances are unclear; does NOT exclude foul play

4.3 Rigor Mortis

Definition: Stiffening of muscles due to ATP depletion → Ca²⁺ accumulation → permanent actomyosin cross-bridges (viscid actomyosin complex).

PhaseTiming
Primary flaccidityImmediately after death
Onset of rigor1–2 hours (range: immediate–3h)
Complete rigor~6–12 hours
Passes off (secondary flaccidity)24–48 hours

Nysten's Law — Order of Rigor Mortis:
Heart → Eyelids → Jaw → Neck → Upper Limbs → Trunk → Lower Limbs
Mnemonic: "He Eats Jam N Unchocolated Toffees Lowly"

4.7 Forensic Entomology
  • Study of insect succession to estimate TSD. Most common fly: Calliphora vicina (bluebottle)

5.2 Legal Requirements for ML Autopsy (India)
  • Written official order from Inquest Officer — Section 174/176 BNSS 2023
  • Body must be identified by police AND ≥2 relatives before autopsy
  • Performed in daylight (artificial light masks jaundice, bruises)
  • All three cavities MUST be opened — partial autopsy is legally void
  • Report in duplicate/triplicate on same day
  • For custodial deaths: video filming mandatory (NHRC guidelines)

A doctor issuing a death certificate for sudden death WITHOUT autopsy risks being charged as an accessory to murder if foul play is later detected.

5.4 Negative Autopsy

An autopsy where cause of death remains undetermined even after exhaustive tests. Occurs in ~2–5% of cases. Common causes: cardiac arrhythmia, drug overdose (small doses), anaphylaxis, epilepsy.

Unit 7 Mechanical Asphyxia +
7.1 Definition & General Signs of Asphyxia

Asphyxia: Condition resulting from interference with respiration, causing excess CO₂ and lack of O₂ in the blood.

  • Tardieu spots: Petechial haemorrhages on pleura, pericardium, conjunctivae — classic sign of asphyxia
  • Cyanosis of lips, nails, skin
  • Congestion and oedema of lungs
  • Engorgement of right heart
  • Facial congestion/oedema (especially hanging, strangulation)
7.2 Hanging

Simon's haemorrhage: Haemorrhage into intervertebral discs (C3–C5) — pathognomonic of hanging.

7.3 Strangulation
Ligature Strangulation
  • Horizontal, continuous mark at or below thyroid cartilage level
  • Petechiae common — face, conjunctivae
  • Hyoid and/or thyroid cartilage fracture common
  • Usually HOMICIDE
Manual Strangulation (Throttling)
  • Always HOMICIDE — cannot be self-inflicted
  • Crescentic bruises and fingernail abrasions around neck
  • Hyoid bone fracture (most commonly greater cornu)
  • Prominent Tardieu spots
  • Prinsloo-Gordon artefact: Postmortem haemorrhage in neck tissues mimicking throttling — must be excluded

Unit 9 Organophosphorus & Aluminium Phosphide Poisoning +
9.1 Organophosphorus Compound (OPC) Poisoning
ParameterDetails
SourceInsecticides (malathion, parathion, chlorpyrifos, DDVP); nerve agents (sarin, VX)
MechanismIrreversible inhibition of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) → ACh accumulation → cholinergic crisis
Fatal dose (malathion)~60–250 mg/kg
Fatal period24–48 hours
RoutesIngestion (most common), inhalation, skin absorption

SLUDGE (Muscarinic features):
S — Salivation   L — Lacrimation   U — Urination   D — Defaecation/Diarrhoea   G — GI cramps   E — Emesis

DUMBELS (Extended Muscarinic):
D-Defaecation · U-Urination · M-Miosis (KEY SIGN — pinpoint pupils) · B-Bradycardia/Bronchospasm/Bronchorrhoea · E-Emesis · L-Lacrimation · S-Salivation/Sweating

Nicotinic: Fasciculations → paralysis; tachycardia; hypertension; mydriasis
CNS: Anxiety → seizures → coma → respiratory failure

Diagnosis & Treatment
AgentDetails
AtropineSPECIFIC antidote (muscarinic). 2–4 mg IV every 5–10 min until atropinisation (dry mouth, tachycardia, dilated pupils). NO dose ceiling in severe poisoning.
Pralidoxime (2-PAM)SPECIFIC (nicotinic). Reactivates AChE if given within 24–48h BEFORE ageing of OPC-AChE bond. 1–2g IV. Does not cross BBB well.
DiazepamControls seizures; 5–10mg IV

"Atropine dries secretions; Pralidoxime reactivates enzyme. Give BOTH. Never delay atropine." Plasma/RBC cholinesterase levels markedly reduced — RBC is more reliable marker.

9.2 Aluminium Phosphide (AlP) Poisoning
ParameterDetails
Commercial namesQuickPhos, Celphos, Phostoxin
Active principlePhosphine gas (PH₃) released on contact with moisture
Fatal dose1 tablet (3g AlP) can be fatal
Fatal period12–24 hours (range: hours–5 days)
MechanismPhosphine inhibits cytochrome c oxidase → blocks mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation → cellular hypoxia + refractory cardiogenic shock
EpidemiologyMost common suicidal poison in North India (Punjab, Haryana)
  • Characteristic sign: Garlic/decaying fish odour on breath and vomitus
  • Refractory cardiogenic shock, severe metabolic acidosis, arrhythmias (VT/VF)
  • Pulmonary oedema, ARDS, multi-organ failure
  • Diagnosis: Silver nitrate paper test — vomitus → black stain (silver phosphide)

NO SPECIFIC ANTIDOTE. Treatment entirely supportive. Gastric lavage with KMnO₄ (1:10,000) — oxidises phosphine. DO NOT use NaHCO₃ (enhances phosphine release). Mortality >50%.

Unit 10 Heavy Metal Poisoning — Arsenic, Lead, Mercury +
10.1 Arsenic Poisoning
ParameterDetails
FormArsenic trioxide (white arsenic, As₂O₃); arsenical pesticides
Fatal dose100–200 mg
Fatal period1–3 days (acute); months–years (chronic)
MechanismInhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase (Krebs cycle); binds sulfhydryl groups
Detection testMarsh Test (James Marsh, 1836) — most reliable; Reinsch test; AAS
Clinical Features
  • Acute: Metallic/garlic taste; "rice-water" vomitus; severe abdominal cramps; diarrhoea; cardiovascular collapse
  • Chronic (Arsenicosis): "Rain-drop" pigmentation; Mees's lines (transverse white nail bands — 1 line = 1 episode); peripheral neuropathy (glove-stocking); alopecia; carcinogen (skin, lung, bladder)

PM Finding: "Red velvety" stomach mucosa — CARDINAL PM finding. Garlic odour; delayed putrefaction (arsenic preserves body). Antidote: BAL/DMSA.

10.2 Lead Poisoning (Plumbism)
FeatureDetails
SourcesPaints, batteries, leaded petrol, pottery, traditional kajal/surma
MechanismInhibits haem synthesis (ALA dehydratase, ferrochelatase) → hypochromic anaemia; disrupts Ca²⁺ signalling
Classic Triad of Chronic Lead Poisoning
  • Anaemia: Hypochromic microcytic; basophilic stippling of RBCs (pathognomonic)
  • Burtonian (blue-black) gum line: Lead sulphide deposited at gum margin (poor oral hygiene)
  • Wrist drop: Radial nerve palsy (extensors); also foot drop — "painter's wrist"
  • Additional: X-ray lead lines at metaphyses; Mees's lines; lead colic; encephalopathy in children

Blood lead level (BLL): normal <5 μg/dL; symptomatic >45 μg/dL. Treatment: DMSA (oral); CaNa₂EDTA (parenteral); BAL for severe encephalopathy.

10.3 Mercury Poisoning
FormClinical Features
Inorganic HgCl₂ (corrosive sublimate)Metallic taste; grayish-white membrane in mouth; bloody diarrhoea; anuria (renal failure); fatal dose 1–4g
Chronic (Acrodynia/Pink Disease)Painful red extremities; photophobia; weakness; neurotoxicity
Organic — Minamata DiseaseMethylmercury bioaccumulation in fish → paresthesias, ataxia, visual/hearing disturbance, congenital defects

10.5 Iron Poisoning
ParameterDetails
SourcesFerrous sulphate tablets, iron-containing multivitamins; most common in young children (accidental ingestion)
Fatal dose>20–60 mg/kg elemental iron
Fatal periodHours to 4 days (biphasic course)
MechanismFree Fe²⁺ → Fenton reaction → free radical (ROS) generation → oxidative damage to GI mucosa, liver, heart, mitochondria; direct corrosive effect on GI epithelium
InvestigationSerum iron >500 μg/dL = severe toxicity; TIBC; abdominal X-ray — iron tablets are radiopaque (visible on plain film)
AntidoteDeferoxamine (DFO) — SPECIFIC chelator; vin rosé/orange urine = ferrioxamine (chelated iron being excreted — confirms antidote is working)
5 Stages of Iron Poisoning
StageTimingFeatures
Stage 1 — GI Phase0–6 hoursHaematemesis, bloody diarrhoea, severe abdominal pain; GI haemorrhage; vomit may be iron-coloured
Stage 2 — Latent Phase6–24 hoursApparent clinical improvement — deceptive; do NOT discharge
Stage 3 — Systemic Toxicity12–48 hoursCardiovascular shock; severe metabolic acidosis (high anion gap); coagulopathy; hepatic failure; coma
Stage 4 — Hepatic Necrosis2–4 daysCentrizonal hepatic necrosis; elevated transaminases; jaundice; coagulopathy
Stage 5 — GI Scarring2–6 weeksPyloric/antral stricture from scar formation → gastric outlet obstruction (late complication)
  • PM findings: GI mucosal haemorrhage and necrosis; centrizonal hepatic necrosis; pulmonary oedema
  • Treatment: Deferoxamine IV (10–15 mg/kg/h) in severe poisoning; whole bowel irrigation with PEG (preferred over lavage); supportive — fluids, vasopressors, FFP

Stage 2 (latent phase) is a medico-legal trap — apparent improvement does NOT mean safety. Patients in stage 2 must be monitored closely and treated if serum iron >500 μg/dL.

11.5 Sulphuric Acid (H₂SO₄) — Oil of Vitriol — Detailed
ParameterDetails
SynonymsOil of vitriol; battery acid; concentrated sulphuric acid (98%)
Fatal dose~5 mL (concentrated); death can occur with smaller amounts
Fatal period1–3 days (may be quicker with perforation/shock)
MechanismExothermic reaction + dehydration of tissues → coagulative necrosis → charring; absorbs water from cells rapidly
PM findingsCharred, leathery, brown-black stomach wall and oesophagus; shrunken blackened mucosa; perforation may be seen
Late complicationOesophageal stricture; pyloric stenosis (from scarring — months later)
Clinical Features
  • Immediate: Intense burning pain from mouth to epigastrium; acrid smoke/fumes if concentrated
  • Lips/buccal mucosa — brown-black charred eschar; contraction of lips
  • Haematemesis (brown-black "coffee-ground" vomitus); severe drooling of saliva
  • Respiratory — laryngeal oedema from fumes/aspiration → stridor, asphyxia
  • Systemic: Shock; metabolic acidosis; haemolysis; renal failure (haemoglobinuria)
  • Skin: Concentrated H₂SO₄ on skin → deep coagulative burn; used in acid-attack (vitriolage)

CONTRAINDICATIONS: Gastric lavage (risk of perforation and re-exposure); emetics; alkali neutralisation (exothermic reaction worsens injury). Dilute immediately with large amounts of WATER or MILK. Morphine/opioids for pain control. Surgical repair of perforations.

11.6 Nitric Acid (HNO₃) — Aqua Fortis — Detailed
ParameterDetails
SynonymsAqua fortis ("strong water"); fuming nitric acid (red/white)
Fatal dose~5 mL (concentrated); 30 mL of dilute
Fatal period1–3 days
MechanismXanthoproteic reaction — HNO₃ nitrates aromatic amino acids (tyrosine, phenylalanine) in tissue proteins → yellow dye product; coagulative necrosis
Characteristic signYellow staining of skin and mucosa (xanthoproteic reaction) — turns orange on alkali exposure
PM findingsYellow-stained GI mucosa; pulmonary oedema (from NO₂ fumes inhalation); coagulative necrosis
Clinical Features
  • Local: Yellow-orange discolouration of skin, lips, mucosa; intense burning pain
  • Fumes (NO₂): Latent pulmonary oedema — may occur 4–24 hours AFTER exposure (silo-filler's disease); cough, dyspnoea, cyanosis
  • Haematemesis; oesophageal burns; laryngeal oedema
  • Systemic: Shock; methaemoglobinaemia (from NO₂ absorption); renal failure

Xanthoproteic reaction is FORENSICALLY IMPORTANT — yellowish staining of victim's skin/clothes in acid-attack cases identifies HNO₃ as the agent. Treatment same as H₂SO₄.

11.7 Hydrochloric Acid (HCl) — Spirit of Salt — Detailed
ParameterDetails
SynonymsSpirit of salt; muriatic acid; "bathroom acid"
Fatal dose~5–15 mL (concentrated 35–37%)
Fatal period1–3 days
MechanismCoagulative necrosis; HCl reacts with haemoglobin → FeCl₃ → greyish-white eschar; fumes (HCl gas) irritate respiratory mucosa
Characteristic signGrey-white eschar on oral mucosa and stomach; less charring than H₂SO₄
PM findingsWhite-grey, contracted mucosa; "shrunken" stomach; less blackening than H₂SO₄; pulmonary changes from fume inhalation
Clinical Features
  • Burning pain; white-grey discolouration of lips, oral mucosa, and tongue
  • Haematemesis; severe drooling; dysphagia
  • Fumes: Choking, cough, lacrimation, rhinorrhoea; pulmonary oedema in severe exposure
  • Systemic: Hypochloraemia; metabolic alkalosis (chloride loss) — paradoxical compared to other acids; shock
  • Late: Oesophageal/pyloric stricture

Concentrated HCl + HNO₃ (3:1 ratio) = Aqua Regia — dissolves gold/platinum. Used in goldsmithing; forensically relevant in acid-attack cases.

11.8 Phenol (Carbolic Acid) — Detailed
ParameterDetails
Common nameCarbolic acid; Lysol (phenol-soap mixture); creosote
Fatal dose~2–15 g (oral); ~2 mL/kg on intact skin (absorbed transdermally)
Fatal periodFew hours to 2 days
MechanismProtein coagulation (white coagulative necrosis); rapidly absorbed from all surfaces (skin, GI, lungs); uncouples oxidative phosphorylation; CNS toxin
Characteristic urineDark olive-green to black urine (phenol → hydroquinone/catechol → dark oxidised products)
Characteristic odourDistinctive carbolic/antiseptic odour on breath, vomitus, and autopsy tissues
PM findingsWhite coagulative necrosis of GI mucosa; carbolic smell throughout autopsy; dark green/black urine in bladder; blanched skin if applied topically
Clinical Features
  • Local GI: White blanching/eschar of oral mucosa; burning pain; dysphagia; haematemesis
  • Skin: White coagulative burn (later gangrenous) — initially PAINLESS (phenol is a local anaesthetic); may be deceptively painless then full thickness burn
  • CNS: Initial stimulation (excitement, convulsions) → depression → coma, respiratory failure
  • Renal: Nephrotoxic; dark urine (phenoluria); haematuria; renal failure
  • CVS: Myocardial depression; hypotension; arrhythmias
  • Methaemoglobinaemia; haemolysis

Phenol — CARD mnemonic:
Carbolic odour | Anaesthetic (skin initially painless) | Renal failure + dark urine | Dark olive-green urine + CNS depression

Treatment: Skin decontamination with PEG-400 (polyethylene glycol) — NOT water alone (water increases absorption). Activated charcoal if ingested. NO emetics. Supportive: airway, N-acetylcysteine (antioxidant). Haemodialysis in renal failure.

11.9 Oxalic Acid — Detailed
ParameterDetails
SourcesRhubarb leaves (highest), sorrel, spinach (small amounts); commercial: leather tanning, metal cleaning agents, bleaching agents
Fatal dose~15–30 g (oral)
Fatal period1–3 hours (rapid — due to acute hypocalcaemia); or 1–2 days
Primary mechanismChelates Ca²⁺ → forms insoluble calcium oxalate → acute hypocalcaemia → tetany, arrhythmia; also direct corrosive damage to GI mucosa; renal toxicity
Renal mechanismCalcium oxalate crystals deposited in renal tubules → acute tubular necrosis → oliguric renal failure
Characteristic findingCalcium oxalate crystals in urine (envelope-shaped/dumbbell); elevated urine oxalate
PM findingsGI corrosion and mucosal whitening; renal tubular necrosis; calcium oxalate crystals in kidneys and urine on microscopy
Clinical Features
  • Local (GI): Burning pain in mouth, oesophagus, stomach; white corrosive burns on mucosa; haematemesis
  • Hypocalcaemia (KEY): Muscle cramps → tetany → carpopedal spasm → laryngospasm → convulsions → cardiac arrhythmias (QT prolongation → VF)
  • Renal: Oliguria/anuria; haematuria; flank pain; oxalate crystals in urine
  • CNS: Headache, confusion, coma
  • Acidosis: High anion gap metabolic acidosis

SPECIFIC treatment: IV Calcium Gluconate (10 mL of 10% solution IV slowly) — corrects hypocalcaemia immediately. Gastric lavage with calcium carbonate solution (lime water) to precipitate soluble oxalate. Haemodialysis for renal failure. NOT to be confused with ethylene glycol poisoning (also causes oxalate crystals — but via metabolic conversion).

11.10 Acetylsalicylic Acid (Aspirin) Poisoning
ParameterDetails
Drug classSalicylate; NSAID; antiplatelet agent
Fatal doseAdults: ~10–30 g; Children: lower (150–200 mg/kg); children far more susceptible
Fatal period12–24 hours (can be longer)
Mechanism (1)Direct respiratory centre stimulation (medullary chemoreceptors) → hyperventilation → respiratory alkalosis (EARLY)
Mechanism (2)Uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation → increased O₂ consumption, increased CO₂, increased heat (hyperthermia), lactic acid production → metabolic acidosis (LATE)
Mechanism (3)Direct GI irritation → haemorrhagic gastritis; antiplatelet → haemorrhage; anti-vitamin K effect (indirect) → coagulopathy
Toxic blood level>300 mg/L (toxic); >500 mg/L (severe); Done nomogram used to assess severity from serum level + time post-ingestion
Clinical Features — Biphasic Acid-Base Disturbance
PhaseTimingFeature
Early (Phase 1)1–6 hoursTinnitus, nausea, vomiting, hyperventilation; respiratory alkalosis (CO₂ blown off)
SalicylismAny timeTinnitus + deafness + sweating + confusion = classic triad of "salicylism"
Late (Phase 2)>6 hoursHigh anion gap metabolic acidosis; hyperthermia; hypoglycaemia (esp. children); pulmonary oedema; cerebral oedema; coma
MixedMost adult casesMixed respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis
  • GI: Epigastric pain, haematemesis, erosive gastritis; GI haemorrhage
  • Renal: Salicylates excreted renally; alkaline urine traps ionised salicylate → enhanced excretion (basis of treatment)
  • Haematological: Antiplatelet + indirect anticoagulation → haemorrhagic diathesis
  • PM findings: Haemorrhagic gastric erosions; cerebral oedema; pulmonary oedema; petechiae

Aspirin Toxicity — TINS mnemonic (Salicylism):
Tinnitus | Impaired hearing | Nausea/vomiting | Sweating
Followed by: Hyperpnoea → Alkalosis → Acidosis → Coma

Treatment: (1) Activated charcoal (within 2h); (2) Urinary alkalinisation with IV NaHCO₃ (pH 7.5–8.0 in urine — ionises salicylate so it cannot be reabsorbed = "ion trapping"); (3) Haemodialysis (SPECIFIC indications: serum salicylate >700 mg/L, pulmonary oedema, renal failure, cerebral oedema, coma, refractory acidosis); (4) Correct hypoglycaemia, coagulopathy, hyperthermia. AVOID forced diuresis alone.

Done Nomogram: Plots serum salicylate level (mg/L) against time post-ingestion to classify toxicity as Mild / Moderate / Severe — used to guide haemodialysis decision. Not validated for chronic salicylism.

Unit 18 Viva Questions, Exam Questions & Spotters +
18.1 Classic High-Yield Viva Q&As
What is the first external sign of putrefaction?
Greenish discolouration over the right iliac fossa (caecum area) — richest bacterial source in the gut.
Why is cadaveric spasm medico-legally important?
It is antemortem — proves the victim was alive at that moment; reflects last voluntary act (e.g., clutching weeds = drowning; gripping pistol = suicide). Unlike rigor mortis, it cannot be artificially produced after death.
Difference between livor mortis and bruise?
Lividity: dependent parts only; early blanching; no tissue infiltration on incision; no swelling. Bruise: any site; never blanches; tissue infiltration on incision; swelling and inflammatory reaction present.
What is the antidote for OPC poisoning?
Atropine (for muscarinic symptoms — no dose ceiling in severe poisoning) + Pralidoxime/2-PAM (for nicotinic symptoms — must give within 24–48h before "ageing" of OPC-AChE bond). Always give BOTH together.
Why is AlP poisoning so dangerous?
No specific antidote; phosphine causes refractory cardiogenic shock via mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase inhibition; mortality >50%; rapidly fatal within hours to 1–2 days. Most common suicidal poison in North India.
Which is the most reliable PM specimen for alcohol analysis?
Vitreous humour — least affected by postmortem redistribution and bacterial production of alcohol during putrefaction.
What is Nysten's Law?
Order of onset of rigor mortis: Heart → Eyelids → Jaw → Neck → Upper limbs → Trunk → Lower limbs. (Reverse order of passing off)
Name the confirmatory tests for antemortem drowning.
1. Diatom test (diatom silica in bone marrow/kidney — gold standard). 2. Cadaveric spasm of hands clutching weeds. 3. Gettler test (chloride difference between heart chambers — historical). 4. Paltauf's haemorrhages (subpleural).
Define brain death in the Indian legal context.
Complete irreversible cessation of all brain functions including brainstem; legally defined under THOTA 1994; requires two independent assessments ≥6h apart by two senior doctors (one neurologist/neurosurgeon). Enables organ donation.
What is Locard's Exchange Principle?
"Every contact leaves a trace." (Edmond Locard) — basis of all trace evidence collection in forensic science. Bidirectional transfer of fibres, hair, pollen, glass, soil between perpetrator, victim, and scene.
What is the PCPNDT Act?
Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act, 1994. Prevents sex determination of foetus and sex selection. Form F mandatory for every procedure. 3–10 years imprisonment for violation. All centres must register with Appropriate Authority.
What is Prinsloo-Gordon artefact?
Postmortem haemorrhage into neck tissues that can be mistaken for throttling — arises from gravitational hypostasis of blood into loose neck tissues after death. Must be excluded before concluding manual strangulation in PM examination.
What is a "negative autopsy"?
An autopsy where the cause of death remains undetermined even after exhaustive post-mortem examination and toxicology. Occurs in ~2–5% of cases. Common causes: cardiac arrhythmia, drug overdose (small doses), anaphylaxis, epilepsy.
Under which law is DNA evidence admissible in India?
Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) 2023 — replaced Indian Evidence Act 1872. Section 79 covers expert evidence including DNA profiling. DNA was first accepted in Indian courts in 1996.
What is the Widmark formula and what is it used for?
Used to back-calculate blood alcohol concentration: A = r × W × C + β₆₀ × t. r = 0.7 (men)/0.6 (women); W = weight (kg); C = BAC (g/100mL×100); β₆₀ = 0.015 g/dL/h elimination rate; t = time elapsed. Used in DUI investigations to determine BAC at earlier time.
